Weak Hero

The year was 1930, and I, Bai Zhuang, was tasked to venture out on an isolated island in the middle of the South China Sea. Years before, I was just an ordinary
boy working for my father. My Father, Li Wang, once was considered a hero by some, he had saved many of his comrades in the First Sino-Japanese war. Despite this,
he was seen as a 'Weak Hero' to many, since he strictly never killed any enemy troops in the battle, he only did if he was forced to or needed to so he could
survive. Hearing this, I was inspired by him, I promised to him that I would become a hero, a strong hero. Knowing my father, he obviously didn't believe me at
first, but over the years, I slowly began to surpass my father, when I finally grew up to be an adult, I had already surpassed my father when it came to raw
strength.

My father had been a young soldier in the Beiyang Fleet during the war of 1894. He had stood on the deck of a wooden-hulled cruiser as Japanese shells
tore through the steel of China's most modern ships. He watched the flagship Dingyuan burn, watched the sea fill with the bodies of men who had never stood a
chance. The Qing Empire had poured silver into a navy that was supposed to protect China's shores, but corruption had hollowed it from within. Shells that did not
fit the guns. Coal that burned too fast. Officers who had bought their ranks. My father carried wounded men through that slaughter, and when the fleet was destroyed,
he carried them across land for weeks, eating roots and drinking from muddy rivers, until they reached safety. He never fired a single shot at the enemy. Not because
he was afraid, but because he had seen what the war truly was. A collapse of a dynasty too old and too rotten to defend its people. Killing a few more boys on the other
side would not have changed that. It would only have added to the pile of grief that China was already drowning in.

The village called him a coward. The foreign newspapers called China the "Sick Man of Asia." The Treaty of Shimonoseki forced China to surrender Taiwan, pay an
indemnity of two hundred million taels, and open more ports to foreign trade. Japan walked away with Korea, with prestige, with the respect of the Western powers.
China walked away humiliated. My father walked away with nothing but the memory of his comrades drowning in yellow water. He never spoke of it unless pressed, and even
then, he would only say: "A hero is not the one who kills the most enemies. A hero is the one who brings the most people home." I did not understand that then. I thought
he was making excuses for his weakness. I was young, and I was angry at the world that had broken him.

My eyes laid upon the calendar, with a bright-red marker, I drew a circle on August 12th, 1910. I did this because this was the exact date I was going to
join a group aiming to fix the world for good. The year before, Japan had formally annexed Korea. Another ancient kingdom swallowed by the empire that had once
been China's student. The news filled me with a cold, burning rage. I wanted to fight. I wanted to prove that China could still produce men of strength. I arrived
at a shady warehouse, at least, that's what it looked like from the outside. Upon entrance, what my eyes had witnessed were almost beyond description. The sight of
the majestic tree in the middle of the light coming from the skylight were enough to make my eyes dwell upon them. Although the interior of this warehouse was stunning
to look at, I didn't arrive here for that reason. Minutes later I had found myself in a dimly lit room with a table in front of me and a chair I was sitting in. Little
did I know I was about to receive my first mission that I am endeavouring to finish.

"All I have to do is to erase the filth in this island" I had dictated to myself, for a first mission, it can't be that hard, right? I was incorrect.
I didn't realize how difficult this would be for the first 3 years. Until then, I had realized the gravity of this whole mission. I had failed to
realize how intricate and tedious it would be. No modern-day cartographer was known to have recorded the island. The smugglers and deserters who
frequented its coast called it Gui Jiao, the Devil's Reef. When I first walked down from the junk boat onto the shallow, warm surf, my knuckles were
firm with arrogance. I had the unbridled strength that my father never dared to use. I was able to break ironwood with a single strike of an open palm,
move through the shadows as easily as smoke, and, unlike Li Wang, I never felt my heart bleed when I knocked men down. I thought that eradication consisted
in the use of force. If you cut off the head of the serpent, the body would wither. I was wrong.

The filth which the organization had sent me to get rid of was not simply a nest for pirates or a base established by a rogue warlord. It was a decaying,
self-supporting ecosystem founded on human suffering. Hidden deep in the jungle canopy of the island was a large shantytown, cut out of the old limestone
caves. A black market hub where opiate dens, illegal stores of weapons, and human trafficking operations all met. The opium came from British India, smuggled
past the treaty ports that foreign gunboats had forced open decades ago. The Mauser pistols carried by the guards were German, sold to Chinese warlords who had
carved up the countryside while the central government collapsed. The refugees in the shantytown were farmers who had lost their land to foreign-backed landlords,
or soldiers whose armies had been scattered by the endless civil wars that had plagued China since the fall of the Qing. It was defended not only by ruthless men
who carried Mauser pistols, but also by a network of desperate refugees, enslaved miners, and orphans who had been taught to slit a man's throat in exchange
for a small amount of rice. Each time I got rid of a syndicate boss, three more brutal hyenas would appear from the mud to take his place. And in the violence
that followed, they would drag dozens of innocent people down with them.

If I destroyed a supply line, then starved people would die within a week. If I burned an opium den, the addicts would wander into the jungle and perish.
The island was not a nest of evil to be cleansed. It was a wound, and violence was only making it fester. It was the same wound that had been carved into
China's body since the Opium Wars, since the Treaty of Nanjing, since the Boxer Rebellion and the Eight-Nation Alliance. Although raw strength might break bones,
it could not repair a broken world. In those terrible early years my father's calm, sad eyes started to appear in my dreams. He hadn't restrained his weapons in
wartime out of fear or weakness. Instead he had done so because he realized how terrible it was to take a life. Mercy was not the lack of strength. It was the
highest degree of strength. While anyone could carry out violence, it took a true giant to bear the consequences of restraint. At that time, in the thick, salty
dusk of 1930, twenty years had elapsed since I had taken the oath beneath that weird, shady room. My youthful arrogance had vanished, leaving behind scars and
a patience earned through hard experience. I had gradually broken down the island's evils, not in the manner of a storm but rather like water wearing away stone.
I got into the inner circles of the organization. I set the captive workers free. I turned the syndicate's greed against itself. The only obstacle left
was tonight.

Above the cliffs, outside the fortress of Commander Chen, the mastermind who had turned this island into an empire of shadows, the lanterns were swinging in the
coastal gale. I wound the rough cloth wraps around my hands and moved out from among the trees into the monsoon rain. I wasn't the same boy who had wanted to show
his father that he was wrong. Tonight I would complete the mission, not as a butcher but as the hero that his heart had always hoped I would become. A hero not of
strength alone, but of understanding. A hero who finally understood that the greatest victory was not in destroying your enemy, but in building something that would
outlast the war itself. My father had known this all along. And tonight, under the black sky of Gui Jiao, I would prove that I had finally learned it too.
